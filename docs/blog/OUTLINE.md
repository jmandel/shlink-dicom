# Blog post outline: your images, in your hands

## How to write this post

- **Plain, clear language.** Write the way Josh writes: first person, short sentences, concrete nouns, numbers with units. Say what happened and what it means; stop when the point is made.
- **No AI slop.** None of the telltale signs:
  - no hype words ("revolutionary", "seamless", "game-changer", "unlock", "powerful", "robust" as filler, "cutting-edge");
  - no false contrasts ("It's not X, it's Y"), no rhetorical questions as transitions, no "Here's the thing";
  - no triplets for rhythm, no "In today's world…", no summary paragraph that repeats the post;
  - no headings that count things; no em-dash chains; no emoji.
- **Headings name content.** "How the files are encrypted", not "The magic under the hood".
- **Every claim is true and checked.**
  - Re-measure every number from the live sites, the corpus, git or the session transcripts, and record where it came from in the figure's source line.
  - Claims about patient access law and how imaging sharing works today need a source. Don't use strawmen.
- **Josh wrote SMART Health Links and the SMART Imaging Access IG.** Don't thank their authors, and don't announce his authorship either.
- **Lead with the story:** patients and their images first, then the idea, then one example traced end to end. Detail comes after.
- **Captions are one plain sentence** saying what to look at, plus a source line where it applies.
- **Who makes each figure:**
  - **Illustrations** (marked *Codex*) are generated by the Codex CLI from the spec, as a detailed image prompt, in the style of the chosen header (`docs/blog/images/header.png`): clean, abstract, whimsical watercolor with a few fine ink lines on mostly white paper, in the SMART palette (purple, orange, red, yellow, green, sky blue, charcoal), never rustic or cluttered.   - **Data charts** (marked *SVG*) are drawn from measured data, never generated, in the house style of the recent posts.

## 1. Opening: images belong in patients' hands

- **The everyday reality, first person.** Imaging still reaches patients as a CD, a portal viewer that shows pictures but won't hand over the files, or nothing at all [[CHECK]].
- **The argument.** Once patients hold their own full-fidelity images, they can do things nobody has to build for them in advance:
  - send them for a second opinion;
  - keep a lifetime record across health systems;
  - show family on a phone;
  - run new software or AI tools on them;
  - donate them to research;
  - and, the subject of this post, share them with a link.
- **What the law already says.** Patients have a right to electronic copies of their records, imaging included, and these are electronic health information under the Cures Act information blocking rules [[CHECK: HIPAA Right of Access covers images in the requested electronic form when readily producible; Cures Act EHI definition and information blocking scope]]. Keep this to two or three plain sentences with links.
- **The project in one paragraph.** To show what becomes possible once patients hold their images, I built a way to share a whole study as a SMART Health Link:
  - every original DICOM file, end-to-end encrypted;
  - opens in a browser without downloading the whole study first;
  - from files, a CD, a patient portal, or a link someone gave you.

## 5. How the files are encrypted and found

- **Files:** one encrypted file per study by default, made of 64 KiB AES-256-GCM records, each record's nonce being its index. A series added later can go in its own file. Update this if the study-file change has landed by publication; otherwise say "per series" [[VERIFY]].
- **Index:** the ImagingStudy index gives every instance's length, so a byte range maps straight to records.
- **What the host sees:** random file names, sizes and byte ranges, and nothing else. At rest it can't see how many series a study has; while someone views, the ranges they read reveal where series and instances begin and end.
- **What's deliberately left out:** compression and ZIP, one line each on why.
- **Storage:** the server can keep files on disk or in S3, and can serve reads itself or redirect them, as a deployment setting, with no change to the protocol [[VERIFY: if Part 2 has landed]].

## 6. Building our own infrastructure

- **Why build it ourselves:**
  - the viewer has to run in any browser on any phone, from untrusted hosts;
  - DICOM in the wild is messy;
  - existing decoders are C libraries with mixed licences.
- **The choice:** pure Rust compiled to WebAssembly for every pixel decoder, with our own DICOM parser, renderer and packer.
- **What exists now,** listed with a number each:
  - **decoders:** uncompressed, RLE, every JPEG mode, JPEG-LS, JPEG 2000 including Part 2, HTJ2K, JPEG XL, float pixel data;
  - **viewer:** a DICOM parser in TypeScript, a WebGL2 renderer with a worker pool, and MPR resampled in patient space;
  - **video:** a remuxer that hands video to the browser, and an LGPL MPEG-2 decoder whose source is served alongside;
  - **sharing:** a streaming encrypting packer with resumable uploads;
  - **server and portal:** the server, and a SMART Imaging Access sandbox portal.
- **Speed against the C libraries:** one line, with a link to the benchmark report [[MEASURE: quiet-machine rerun]].

## 7. How we made it trustworthy

- **The method, as concrete practices.** Each gets a short paragraph and an example:
  1. **Oracle first.** Build the test corpus and the reference decoders before writing a decoder. Every case is checked against independent references, and synthetic seeds are their own truth.
  2. **Rulings, written down.** When references disagree, record why and test against a repaired copy. Example: pydicom applying Planar Configuration to JPEG output where the standard says it doesn't apply.
  3. **The real world as a test.** A census of where clinical imaging comes from, then the NCI Imaging Data Commons:
     - 1,000 of 1,000 random files pass;
     - a stratified long tail of 9,557 files, chosen for rare combinations, finds the edge cases;
     - slides too large to decode whole are checked frame by frame.
  4. **Fuzzing** for every new entropy decoder.
  5. **Trust but re-run.** No agent's "done" was accepted: the coordinator re-ran every check suite, compared files byte for byte, and tested deploys from a clean clone in the target image.
  6. **People review what matters most.** De-identification was checked by an independent search for identifiers plus a person looking at every series; the coordinator caught a real insurer name left on a reconstructed form.
  7. **Infer only with margin.** Missing attributes are inferred only when the evidence wins clearly (a 1.25× smoothness margin), and everything else is refused with a reason.

## 9. What it's like building this with agents

- **The setup:**
  - **The coordinator:** one Claude Code session that planned, dispatched, verified and committed.
  - **The workers:** about 25 Claude subagents, each owning a directory.
  - **pi (Gemini):** two sessions in tmux. One built the first vertical slice; one wrote the study-file plan and drafted this post.
  - **Codex:** made the illustrations.
  - **Survival kit:** a coordination file and a journal kept the work going across context compactions.
- **Josh's role, in his own words:** short questions that cut layers.
  - "What's the zip buying us?"
  - "Why use HKDF at that point?"
  - "Is this suggestion only to rescue slides that lack metadata that could be present in DICOM?"
- **Direction and taste:**
  - "come back at the proposal that addresses everything we plausibly can";
  - "keep our code clean and to the point";
  - "Rather than dropping data… create a synthetic equivalent";
  - the no-slop copy rules.
- **What the agents did well:** parallel workstreams that would each have been a person's month, done in a day; exhaustive checking; patience with the long tail.
- **What went wrong,** told plainly with one example each:
  - **Confident words standing in for decisions:** "out of scope", and an overstated CORS problem.
  - **An agent's "done" isn't verified:** the build that only worked on one machine; the download crash no check covered.
  - **Shared-state collisions:** a commit that swept in another agent's staged files, builds deleting the site mid-test, one agent overwriting another's file.
  - **Infrastructure:** exe.dev hosts that dropped SSH, solved only when Josh moved regions.
  - **A forgotten background job:** the header image waited hours on input.
- **The numbers** [[MEASURE all]]:
  - Josh's messages, words and estimated attention hours;
  - the agents' working hours, including hours while Josh was away;
  - subagent count;
